== 5f5b199c
The last main topic of this course is the Ethics of AI. This topic is last, but it is not less important than the other topics. Ethics is not an optional, "nice-to-know" topic. Some years ago, many people (and, to be honest, also we) thought that it was. Now AI has a larger effect on society every year, and thus people see and discuss more ethical problems.

== fd57c0c1
The text that you read now is not complete. We continue to change it. This shows that the field changes quickly, because more and more people discuss it.

== 51f45d76
This often occurs: AI specialists or other technologists find a problem and think that they are the first people who ever thought about it. Then they do not see the work of other people. Some of these people studied the same problems for many years, sometimes for decades.

== 3f0b4dea
When you work on ethical problems of technology, try to find earlier work on the topic. This can be difficult, because people possibly used different words for the same things. But it is always good to try. Probably you are not the first person who found the problem. Look specially for work in <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_and_technology_studies">Science and Technology Studies (STS)</a>.

== f1ca51db
AI and ethics meet in different ways. One topic is <b>machine ethics</b>. Machine ethics builds computer systems that behave ethically, or that have a moral code in a very literal sense. Possibly you know <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Laws_of_Robotics"> the Three Laws of Robotics</a>. They are from the 1942 short story Runaround by the science fiction author Isaac Asimov. Machine ethics studies rules of this type, which people could possibly put into AI systems.

== f5f76f19
Machine ethics is a correct scientific field. But it has no connection to the AI systems that exist now and to their effect on society. Modern AI systems cannot directly apply a rule like <i>"A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm"</i> (the First Law of Robotics by Asimov). Before the system can apply it, the system must reliably identify what is injury and what is harm.

== 7c87526b
Thus, this part of the course is <i>not</i> about machine ethics. It is about the <b>ethics of AI</b>: the larger view of the social and ethical problems that the use of AI systems causes. We do not look only at the technical parts of an AI system. We look at the full socio-technical context of the AI system or systems. This context also includes the people who use the systems and the people who feel their effects.

== 8919b429
As we discussed, people do not agree on one definition of AI. Thus, it is difficult to write a clear regulation for the use of AI. In our personal opinion, the law <i>should not</i> regulate AI as a separate thing. The law should regulate AI together with all other technology. In many cases, the law should regulate it as a human activity, not as a technology.

== 4b11e1ae
Ethically or legally, why is it important if a person does a thing with AI or without AI? We know of no case where the use of AI must make a thing legal or illegal. If a law depends on whether a thing is AI or not, people will probably find loopholes in the definition of AI.

== 9cd384a4
But AI systems can increase some possible harms to people. Examples are mass surveillance, deanonymization (people in anonymous data become known), loss of accountability and difficult questions about authorship. Thus, different regulators, most importantly the European Union, decided to make AI regulation. This decision is already made, and we cannot change it. Lawyers will have more work: they must find when the AI regulation applies and when it does not apply. They must also find how it agrees with the regulation that already exists for each sector. But this is the normal work of lawyers... 🤷

== 685514c4
<b>The AI Act puts AI into classes by its risk</b>

== 6653de4a
AI with an unacceptable risk is prohibited (for example, social scoring systems and manipulative AI).

== 621dea23
Most of the text is about high-risk AI systems. The Act regulates them.

== 9899a76f
A smaller section is about limited-risk AI systems. They have lighter transparency obligations: developers and deployers must make sure that end users know that they use AI (for example, chatbots and deepfakes).

== 3fe4d075
AI with minimal risk has no specific obligations under the Act. Most AI applications on the EU single market are in this class, for example video games with AI and spam filters. Only general rules apply to them. An example is AI literacy for the staff of providers and deployers (Art. 4). Providers can also follow voluntary codes of conduct. Generative AI has separate rules: the transparency obligations above and the rules for general-purpose AI models below.

This is a simplified overview of the summary on this page. It is not legal advice. The class of a real system depends on its intended purpose and on the exceptions in the Act. Thus, read the text of the Act itself.

== 741c9578
The Moral Machine is an online platform. MIT leads it. It shows people simplified dilemmas, similar to the “trolley problem”, as crashes of autonomous vehicles (for example: turn or stay in the lane; save the passengers or the pedestrians). It records the choices of the people. Here is the article about <a href="https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0637-6">the Moral Machine Experiment</a> in Nature (October 2018). This main publication is Awad et al., Nature (2018), “The Moral Machine experiment”. It reports about 39.61 million decisions from participants in 233 countries and territories (in ten languages). It analyses (a) the global preferences of all participants together, (b) the differences between demographic groups and (c) the patterns and clusters of countries.

== d434be72
Limits and criticism: these results mainly describe what people said. They show what survey participants say that they would choose when the questions are simplified. They do not show what is ethically correct or legally acceptable. The scenarios also remove much of real driving: uncertainty, what is possible, the duty of care, and the main aim of safety engineering, which is to decrease the total risk. Also, the participants selected themselves, and some populations have many more participants than others. Thus, the interpretation “this is what a country believes” is weak. The forced choices can also make preferences look stronger than they are. In a reply in Nature, <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-020-1987-4">Bigman and Gray (2020)</a> let participants also choose to treat all people equally. In most scenarios, most participants chose this. (The authors of the Moral Machine replied in the same issue with more data, and they did not agree.)

== c28c5e47
Also, even with perfect data about preferences, it is difficult to make rules from it. Who decides? With which authority? How do we solve conflicts? These are questions of legitimacy and governance. The preference of the majority in a survey is not automatically a good basis for policy.

== c30f7b84
Generative AI has benefits. But people are also worried about misuse and errors. In some areas, the legal frameworks are older than the technology. The OECD works with governments to make policies for the ethical and responsible use of generative AI. These policies try to decrease the risks and make sure that the technology helps society. (This section is adapted and extended from the <a href="https://oecd.ai/en/genai/issues/risks-and-unknowns"> OECD Policy Observatory for AI</a>.)

== ea45ec41
Sometimes large language models (textual generative AI) give outputs that are incorrect but look correct. This is a hallucination. The model does not do this intentionally. It can occur also when the correct answer is in the training data. The reason: training teaches models to make text that looks probable, and some training and evaluation methods give a confident guess a better score than an answer that says "I am not sure". Thus, the models learn to guess. Hallucinations spread incorrect information. They also mislead users who use the output and do not check it. (<i>Model collapse</i> is a different problem: models that train on much AI-generated data can lose information about the original data, step by step, and become worse.)

== 1cd66073
A toy version of model collapse. The “model” is a probability distribution over 20 categories. Each generation of the model is fitted to samples from the model of the previous generation. When a rare category gets no samples, it disappears and never comes back. Thus, the tails of the original distribution disappear first, as Shumailov et al. describe in <a href="https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07566-y" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Nature 631 (2024)</a>. Larger samples make the loss slower, but they do not stop it. The total variation distance is the largest difference between the probabilities that the model and the original give to a set of categories. It is 0 when the two are the same, and 1 when they have no overlap. When each generation also gets new real data, lost categories can come back. Then the model moves around the original and does not collapse. Shumailov et al. found that their language models became worse more slowly when they kept 10&nbsp;% of the original data. <a href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Gerstgrasser et al. (2024)</a> found that collapse does not occur when you add synthetic data to the real data and do not replace the real data.

== fb98e90b
Generative AI makes the creation of content more efficient. But it also has risks that we must examine carefully. One large risk is fake or misleading content. For example, people can use generative AI to make images or videos that look real but are completely false. They can use them to spread disinformation or to deceive people. Thus, it becomes more difficult to find fake digital media and to verify real media.

== c7460c94
Generative AI causes problems with intellectual property rights, specially about:

== 7e52ba15
content in the training data that has no licence,

== 4b1e7561
possible infringement of copyright, patents and trademarks by things that the AI makes,

== 502198ee
and who owns the works that AI makes.

== 713b0cb5
In Europe and in the US, people do not agree if companies can legally train ML models on copyrighted material. In the US, people started several lawsuits against companies. They say that these companies trained their models on copyrighted works without permission. The first decisions came in 2025. In <i>Thomson Reuters v. Ross Intelligence</i>, a court did not accept the fair-use defence: Ross trained a competing legal research tool on copied Westlaw headnotes. In <i>Bartz v. Anthropic</i> and <i>Kadrey v. Meta</i>, courts decided that it was fair use to train language models on books, but for narrow reasons. In <i>Bartz</i>, this was not true for pirated copies in a central library. In <i>Kadrey</i>, the reason was that the authors did not show harm to the market for their books. Many cases are not yet decided. Their results will have an effect on the full generative AI industry, from start-ups to multinational technology companies. In the EU, text and data mining of works that you can access legally is allowed, unless the rightholder reserved the right (for example in a machine-readable form; Article 4 of the Copyright Directive (EU) 2019/790). The AI Act says that providers of general-purpose AI models must respect these reservations.

== df68d6ab
Generative AI will probably change labour markets and jobs. But it is not yet known how, and experts do not agree. Generative AI can possibly do tasks that humans usually do. Then some people in some industries and professions can lose their jobs.

== 4dfbd916
Generative AI can possibly automate some jobs or remove them. But it can also change jobs that exist. Then humans can possibly do tasks more efficiently and find new creative possibilities. This change will also change which skills people need.

== f102550d
To decrease these risks, we must combine technical solutions, policy frameworks and responsible practices. Then generative AI can help society and cause as little harm as possible.

== 99f4cab8
Generative AI needs very much computing power and uses natural resources. Thus, it has a large effect on the environment (a large ecological footprint). If people use generative AI without good control in areas such as climate modelling and environmental simulations, it can accidentally make ecological problems worse and make conservation work less effective.

== 49070edc
AI can analyse large quantities of data and find valuable information that humans cannot see. But there is a risk: AI can make the biases in the training data stronger. If the training data has biases, for example racial or gender stereotypes, the generative AI model can accidentally give biased outputs, for example misleading or inappropriate content. This can continue and even increase the inequality and discrimination in society.

== c53e52bd
Generative AI also causes privacy problems. These models train on large quantities of data. Thus, they can accidentally learn private or sensitive information and show it again. For example, a language model that trained on text data can show personal details or confidential information.

== 9ac72db4
In the near future, generative AI can make problems worse. Synthetic content of different quality and accuracy spreads quickly in digital spaces. Then people use this content to train the next generative AI models, and the problem becomes larger each time (a vicious cycle). In the longer term, new behaviours can possibly occur, for example more agency, a search for power, and hidden sub-goals that help to reach a main objective. These behaviours possibly do not agree with human values and intentions. If they occur, they can cause systemic harm and make people collectively lose power. Because of these risks, too much reliance, trust and dependency on AI can cause deep, long-term harm to societies. Also, if only a small number of multinational technology companies and governments have the AI resources, the world can become unbalanced.

== abb7df05
An explanation can be <b>global</b> or <b>local</b>. A global explanation shows which features are important for the model in general. A local explanation shows the reason for one decision, for example why the model rejected this applicant. Some models are interpretable by design, for example a short decision tree or a linear model with few features. For the other models, there are post-hoc methods (methods that you use after training), for example LIME, SHAP, saliency maps and counterfactual explanations ("you would have been accepted with an income of 40&nbsp;000&nbsp;€"). <b>Permutation importance</b> is one of the simplest global methods. Mix the values of one feature column of the test data in a random sequence. This breaks the connection between the feature and the labels. Then measure how much the accuracy decreases.

== 795a8d9c
Here it audits a hiring model. The model trained on decisions from the past, and these decisions were biased against one group. The model does <i>not</i> get the group, and skill has the same distribution in the two groups.

== 34f3f2be
The explanation shows the problem. The model uses the postcode very much, and the postcode has a correlation with the group (correlation 0.86). The model hires 67&nbsp;% of group 0, but only 35&nbsp;% of group 1. The protected attribute was removed from the inputs (people sometimes call this <i>fairness through unawareness</i>), but the bias stayed: the model made it again from a proxy. When we train the model again without the postcode, the hire rates become almost equal here (48 and 50&nbsp;%). But the accuracy decreases from 0.85 to 0.75. The reason is that we measure the accuracy against the same biased decisions from the past. Thus, a fairer model can look worse on biased test data. Real data usually has many weak proxies, not one strong proxy. This is why audits measure the outcomes for each group, and do not only delete columns.

== f1debf99
Two warnings about explanations. First, they describe the <b>model</b>, not the world: when a feature has a high importance, this does not mean that the feature causes the outcome. Second, correlated features share their importance or hide the importance of each other. Thus, an explanation method can be misleading exactly in the cases where proxies are important.

== 23812d20
A famous study of kidney stone treatments (Charig et al., <i>BMJ</i> 292, 1986) compared open surgery with a less invasive "keyhole" procedure (PCNL):

== bf00acd5
Open surgery is more successful for small stones <i>and</i> for large stones. But for all patients together, it is less successful. This is <b>Simpson's paradox</b>. The explanation: doctors used open surgery more often for large stones, and large stones are more difficult to treat. Thus, the stone size has an effect on the choice of treatment and also on the outcome. A variable of this type is a <b>confounder</b>. The question "Which treatment must a new patient get?" asks what would occur if we <i>intervened</i> and gave all patients the same treatment. We write this as <span class="math">P(\text{success} \mid do(\text{treatment}))</span>. The causal graph has three arrows: stone size <span class="math">\to</span> treatment, stone size <span class="math">\to</span> success and treatment <span class="math">\to</span> success. (This is a Bayesian network as in Week 3, but each arrow means "causes".) With this graph, the <b>adjustment formula</b> calculates the average of the success rates for each stone size, weighted by the size distribution of all patients (51&nbsp;% small, 49&nbsp;% large):

== 02893a0d
and for PCNL <span class="math">0.87 \times 0.51 + 0.69 \times 0.49 \approx 0.78</span>. When we include the confounder in the calculation, open surgery is the better treatment. But the answer is only as good as the graph. The graph assumes that stone size is the only common cause. A randomized controlled trial does not need this assumption: it gives the treatments at random, and thus it removes all arrows into the treatment. Judea Pearl calls this the <b>ladder of causation</b>. It has three levels: seeing (association), doing (intervention) and imagining (counterfactuals). An example of a counterfactual question from fairness research: "Would this applicant have been hired if they belonged to the other group?"

== 100e59a0
In science, people use AI more and more as a fast, learned replacement for expensive experiments or simulations. <b>AlphaFold2</b> predicts the 3D structure of a protein from its amino-acid sequence. In the blind CASP14 assessment of 2020, its predictions in most cases were almost as good as structures from experiments. DeepMind and EMBL-EBI published more than 200 million predicted structures, free for all. Demis Hassabis and John Jumper got half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for it. (David Baker got the other half for computational protein design.) <b>GraphCast</b> learned weather forecasting from historical weather data of many decades. It makes a global forecast for 10 days in less than a minute. On 90&nbsp;% of 1380 verification targets, it was more accurate than ECMWF's HRES, the most accurate operational deterministic system.

== 743b8bee
The ethical and methodological questions of this week also apply here. A learned model trains on the past. Thus, it can fail on rare extreme events, and these are often the most important events. Its predictions are hypotheses that we must test, not measurements. When results look very good, examine them as carefully as any other scientific claim: how did the authors validate the predictions, and against what?
