    What is AI?

    Artificial intelligence is not defined by one universally accepted sentence. It is a field of research and engineering whose boundaries move as technologies become familiar. Search, optical character recognition and speech recognition were once emblematic AI problems; when they became reliable, they increasingly came to be treated as ordinary computing infrastructure. This is sometimes called the AI effect.

    It is therefore useful to distinguish an operational definition, which tells us what systems fall within a technical or regulatory scope, from a philosophical definition, which asks what intelligence, understanding or mind actually are. The first can guide engineering and governance without settling the second.

    A contemporary working definition

    The updated OECD definition and the EU AI Act describe an AI system as a machine-based system that operates with varying levels of autonomy and may adapt after deployment. For explicit or implicit objectives, it infers from its inputs how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments.

    The important words are machine-based, objectives, inference, outputs and influence. The definition does not require the system to resemble a human, be conscious, or use one particular technique such as machine learning.

    Model, system and application

    An AI model is a learned or programmed mathematical structure that transforms inputs into outputs. An AI system includes the model together with interfaces, data flows, tools, rules, monitoring, people and a context of use. For example, a language model is not by itself a clinical triage system. The latter also includes the user interface, patient data, prompts, retrieval tools, escalation procedures and human responsibility. This distinction matters because capabilities belong partly to models, while consequences and accountability arise from systems in use.

    Four ways of asking whether a system is intelligent

    What should an AI system do to count as intelligent? Should it think like a person, or is it enough for it to behave successfully? Should we compare it with humans, who sometimes make mistakes, or with a standard of rationality?

    Russell and Norvig organize approaches to AI around two distinctions:

    • Thinking versus acting: thinking concerns the processes inside the system and how it arrives at a conclusion; acting concerns its observable behaviour and what it achieves.
    • Human versus rational standards: a human standard compares the system with how people actually think or behave; a rational standard asks whether its reasoning is correct or its actions are expected to achieve its objectives.

    Combining these distinctions gives four perspectives. These are not four types of AI system. They are four ways of defining the goal of AI research and deciding what evidence would count as success.

    Human standard · Thinking

    Thinking humanly

    Question: Does the system solve problems in a way that resembles human thinking?

    This approach studies the process, not just the answer. A person and a computer might solve the same puzzle correctly but use very different steps. A cognitive model aims to reproduce aspects of how people actually think, including their response times and characteristic mistakes.

    Example: a model of human memory predicts that people recall frequently used words more easily than rarely used words. Researchers compare its predictions with experiments involving people.

    Typical evidence: psychological experiments, observations of human problem-solving and cognitive models.

    Rational standard · Thinking

    Thinking rationally

    Question: Does the system reason correctly from the information it has?

    Formal logic provides rules for checking whether conclusions follow from premises. In deductive reasoning, if the premises are true and the reasoning is valid, the conclusion must also be true.

    Example: all mammals are warm-blooded; a whale is a mammal; therefore, a whale is warm-blooded. The conclusion follows from the two premises.

    Correct reasoning alone is not enough: a premise may be wrong, relevant facts may be missing, or the system may lack time to work through all possibilities.

    Typical evidence: formal logic and proofs that conclusions follow from stated premises.

    Human standard · Acting

    Acting humanly

    Question: Does the system behave in a way that resembles human behaviour?

    Here the focus is observable behaviour. The system does not necessarily need to use the same internal processes as a person; it needs to produce sufficiently human-like behaviour.

    Example: in the Turing test, a human interrogator exchanges written messages with an unseen human and an unseen machine and tries to distinguish them from their answers.

    Human-like behaviour is not necessarily optimal. People make errors, take shortcuts and sometimes act inconsistently. Resembling a human and performing a task well are different goals.

    Typical evidence: the Turing test and studies comparing human and machine behaviour.

    Rational standard · Acting

    Acting rationally

    Question: Does the system choose actions expected to achieve its objective, given what it knows and the resources available?

    An agent receives information from an environment and acts in that environment. A performance measure specifies what counts as doing well.

    Example: a robot vacuum uses its sensors to decide where to move. Its performance measure might reward cleaning the floor while considering time, energy use and avoiding collisions. It does not need to clean the way a human would.

    Rationality does not mean always succeeding or having perfect information. A sensible action can have a poor outcome because of an unexpected event. The question is whether the choice was reasonable given the information and resources available at the time.

    Typical evidence: task performance relative to a stated objective under specified conditions.

    One example seen from four perspectives

    Consider a navigation app finding a route through a city:

    • Thinking humanly: does it plan the route using processes that resemble human planning?
    • Thinking rationally: do its conclusions follow correctly from the information and rules it uses?
    • Acting humanly: do its route choices resemble those a person would make?
    • Acting rationally: is its chosen route expected to achieve the stated objective, such as arriving quickly while avoiding tolls?

    The app may find an excellent route without planning like a person. Equally, it may reason correctly from its map but suggest a poor route because the map is outdated. Human similarity, correct reasoning and successful action are different things.

    Russell and Norvig use the rational-agent perspective as an organizing framework for much of AI: an agent receives information from an environment and selects actions according to a performance measure, given limited information, computation and time. This is useful for designing and evaluating systems, but it is not a complete theory of human intelligence. What counts as a good action also depends on how we define the objective and what the performance measure includes.

    Reference: Stuart Russell and Peter Norvig, Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach, 4th edition, Section 1.1.

    Three different questions
    What counts as an AI system?
    An operational or legal scope question. The OECD definition and EU AI Act answer this without defining a mind or measuring intelligence.
    What do we mean by intelligence?
    A conceptual and measurement question. Different theories emphasize goal achievement, reasoning, learning efficiency or adaptation.
    What evidence would justify calling a system AGI?
    A threshold and classification question. There is no agreed test, so proposed frameworks operationalize different aspects.

    These answers are complementary rather than competing definitions. A system may fall within a legal definition of AI without being highly intelligent, and a general-purpose model is not automatically AGI.

    Narrow AI, general-purpose models and AGI

    Term
    Useful meaning
    Example or qualification
    Narrow AI
    Strong capability in a bounded task or domain.
    A chess engine, spam filter or medical-image classifier can exceed human performance without broad competence.
    General-purpose AI model
    A model that can perform many distinct tasks or support many downstream applications.
    This is a category in the EU AI Act. It does not imply general intelligence.
    Artificial general intelligence (AGI)
    A contested research concept for broad, high-level competence across tasks and contexts.
    There is no agreed scientific test or legal definition.

    AGI is not one additional legal category. It is a contested claim about the breadth and level of a system's intelligence. Different frameworks operationalize that claim differently. The aspects below should therefore be read as a comparison of proposals, not as an agreed checklist that a system either passes or fails.

    Aspects used to operationalize general intelligence

    Goal achievement across environments
    An intelligent agent succeeds at goals over a wide range of environments rather than one predefined task.

    Breadth or generality

    Depth or performance

    Morris et al. (2024)
    AGI is not a synonym for everything dramatic
    • AGI is not the same as consciousness: broad task performance does not establish subjective experience.
    • AGI is not the same as autonomy: a capable model may have little independent control, while a simple system may operate autonomously.
    • AGI is not the same as superintelligence: generality concerns breadth; superintelligence concerns performance far beyond humans.
    • AGI need not reproduce human cognition: similar performance can arise from different mechanisms.

    What would count as convincing evidence?

    No framework above supplies a complete test. A benchmark samples behaviour under specified conditions; it does not measure an entire mind. Strong scores can reflect training-data contamination, prompt design, tool access or extensive scaffolding. When evaluating an AGI claim, ask:

    • Breadth: are the tasks genuinely diverse rather than minor variations of one task family?
    • Novelty: were the test problems unavailable during training, and do they require new abstractions?
    • Efficiency: how much data, computation, prior knowledge and human guidance were required?
    • Transfer: does competence survive changes in representation, domain or objective?
    • Reliability: is performance consistent, or based on occasional successful demonstrations?
    • Scaffolding: what comes from the model, and what comes from prompts, retrieval, tools or human operators?
    • Autonomy: is independent action being reported separately from capability?

    A brief history of AI: origins, optimism and setbacks

    AI developed alongside computer science. Alan Turing's work connected the idea of mechanical computation with questions about intelligent behaviour. John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon used the term artificial intelligence in their 1955 proposal for the Dartmouth summer research project, held in 1956. Its ambition was that aspects of learning and intelligence could be described precisely enough for a machine to simulate them.

    Games, theorem proving and puzzles provided early testbeds: their states, legal moves and goals could be specified explicitly. Search and planning produced important advances, including methods related to the A* search and alpha-beta pruning studied in this course. Success in these restricted settings encouraged optimism about much broader machine intelligence, but moving from a well-defined problem to an open, changing world proved much harder.

    Good Old-Fashioned AI (GOFAI) versus modern AI

    GOFAI refers to the classical symbolic approach: represent knowledge as explicit symbols, facts and rules, then manipulate those representations through logical inference, search or planning. For example, an expert system might combine rules elicited from specialists to derive a recommendation. The difficulty is not only executing the rules: someone must identify relevant knowledge, encode it and keep it up to date.

    The earlier course material used modern AI to emphasize methods that cope with uncertain, incomplete and noisy information. Probabilistic models represent degrees of uncertainty and update beliefs as evidence arrives. Machine learning estimates patterns or model parameters from data; deep learning also learns useful internal representations. These are related developments, but reasoning under uncertainty and learning from data are different capabilities: a probabilistic model can be specified by an expert, and a learned model need not explicitly represent uncertainty.

    QuestionClassical symbolic AI / GOFAIProbabilistic and learning-based approaches
    How is knowledge represented?Explicit symbols, facts, relations and rules.Probability distributions, statistical models or learned representations.
    Where does knowledge come from?Mainly from people encoding a domain and its rules.From expert assumptions, data, or a combination of both.
    How are conclusions produced?Inference, search and planning over explicit representations.Probabilistic inference or predictions from learned models.
    What works well?Clearly specified constraints, structured problems and inspectable reasoning steps.Noisy observations, pattern recognition and tasks where writing every rule is impractical.
    What can go wrong?Brittleness, missing knowledge, expensive maintenance and large search spaces.Biased or inadequate data, distribution shift, uncertain reliability and difficult explanations.

    A small GOFAI example: classifying one email

    Observed facts: the sender is unknown; the message mentions a lottery; it asks for an urgent money transfer.

    Rule 1: IF the sender is unknown AND the message urgently requests money, THEN mark the message as suspicious.

    Rule 2: IF a suspicious message mentions a lottery, THEN classify it as spam.

    Inference: Rule 1 derives suspicious; Rule 2 then derives spam. Every step can be inspected.

    Limitation: “You won a prize” or a new fraud vocabulary may evade the rules until someone updates the knowledge base.

    A learned classifier approaches the same task differently: it estimates patterns from labelled messages, while a probabilistic classifier can estimate the probability of spam given the observed words. The symbolic approach depends on the coverage of its rules; the learned approach depends on the quality of its data and whether the learned patterns transfer to new messages.

    This contrast describes broad emphases, rather than two mutually exclusive eras. Neural learning already existed in the early decades of AI, symbolic methods remain useful today, and systems can combine learned perception with explicit rules, search or planning. “Modern” does not mean that a system understands the world or will necessarily generalize reliably.

    Concrete GOFAI systems: successes and limits

    GOFAI was not one algorithm, and it was not simply a collection of failures. It produced striking demonstrations and useful deployed systems. Its characteristic difficulty was that knowledge valid in a carefully delimited domain could become brittle when the domain, vocabulary or context changed.

    SystemWhat it didWhat it teaches us
    Logic Theorist (1956) Newell, Simon and Shaw used heuristic search to prove theorems from Principia Mathematica. Reasoning can be formulated as selective search through a very large space of possibilities.
    SHRDLU (1968-70) Winograd's program answered questions and followed natural-language instructions in a simulated blocks world. Language, planning and reference can look remarkably intelligent when the world is small and fully modelled.
    Shakey (1966-72) SRI's mobile robot combined perception, a world model and symbolic planning to move through rooms and manipulate objects. A high-level goal can be decomposed into actions, but only with a simplified environment and slow, carefully engineered perception.
    MYCIN (1970s) A rule-based expert system recommended antibiotics for bacterial infections and represented uncertainty with certainty factors. Specialist knowledge can be encoded and explanations can cite rules, but acquiring, validating and maintaining the knowledge is expensive.
    XCON (1980s) DEC used thousands of rules to configure orders for VAX computer systems. GOFAI could create substantial commercial value in a stable domain while revealing the maintenance cost of a large rule base.

    Many contemporary systems combine approaches: learned perception or language models supply judgments, while search, programs, tools, rules or constraints organize multi-step behaviour. This family is often called neuro-symbolic AI, although the term covers several different architectures.

    AI winters: when expectations exceeded results

    An AI winter is a period of reduced funding, commercial investment and confidence in AI after expectations are disappointed. It does not mean that research stopped. Dates vary across accounts and countries, but two broad periods are commonly distinguished.

    The first AI winter: roughly the mid-1970s to the early 1980s

    Early demonstrations had encouraged claims that general machine intelligence was close. In practice, programs struggled with real-world language, perception and the rapid growth of search spaces. Computing resources and available data were limited. Criticism of simple perceptrons also weakened confidence in neural-network research, although the limitations of those models were not a proof that all neural networks were incapable. The 1973 Lighthill Report criticized progress in the UK, and changes in funding priorities in the UK and US contributed to a broader loss of support.

    The second AI winter: roughly the late 1980s to the mid-1990s

    The commercial expansion of expert systems brought renewed optimism. Some systems were useful in bounded domains, but acquiring and maintaining their knowledge was expensive, and they often struggled with unfamiliar situations. Disappointing returns, together with the collapse of the specialized Lisp-machine market as general-purpose computers became competitive, undermined investment. These problems concern the limits of particular technologies and business expectations; they do not imply that every expert system failed or that symbolic reasoning became useless.

    Renewed growth: probability, data and learned representations

    From the late 1980s and through the 1990s, probabilistic reasoning and statistical machine learning gained influence. Larger datasets, improved algorithms and increased computing power later supported the resurgence of neural networks as deep learning. Large pretrained models subsequently enabled reuse across many tasks and modalities. These developments overlap: they are not a single invention that suddenly ended a winter.

    What should we learn from AI winters?

    Success on a restricted task does not establish general intelligence. Ask what a system demonstrates, under which conditions it works, what resources it needs and whether claims go beyond the evidence. The history of AI connects directly to our philosophical questions: successful behaviour alone does not settle understanding, consciousness or the nature of human skill.

    Connection to the course: Week 1 introduces search and games; Week 2 develops reasoning under uncertainty with probability and Bayesian methods. Later weeks explore learning and neural networks. The aim is to understand what each approach contributes and how they can be combined. For further historical context, see Russell and Norvig, the introduction to Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach.

    On the philosophy of AI

    Philosophy helps separate questions that are easily conflated. Behaviour asks what a system can do. Mechanism asks how it produces that behaviour. Understanding asks whether its symbols or outputs have meaning for the system. Phenomenology asks whether the system has subjective experience. Evidence bearing on one question does not automatically settle the others.

    Turing: operationalizing intelligent behaviour

    In Computing Machinery and Intelligence (1950), Alan Turing proposed an imitation game. A human interrogator exchanges written messages with hidden participants and attempts to determine which is human and which is a machine. Turing's move was methodological: rather than first defining an invisible inner property called thinking, examine observable linguistic performance. Passing such a test would be significant evidence of conversational competence, but it would not by itself explain the mechanism, establish consciousness or prove that the machine thinks in the way humans do.

    Searle: syntax is not automatically semantics

    John Searle's Chinese Room thought experiment asks us to imagine a person who does not understand Chinese but follows a rulebook for transforming Chinese symbols. To people outside the room, the answers may appear fluent and appropriate. Searle argues that formal symbol manipulation—syntax—is not by itself sufficient for meaning or semantics. The argument targets the claim that running the right program is sufficient for understanding; it does not show that AI systems cannot be useful or behave intelligently. Replies include the systems reply (the whole system of person, rulebook and room understands, even if the person does not), the robot reply (the symbols get their meaning once the program is connected to the world through sensors and motors) and the brain-simulator reply (a program that simulates the neuron firings of a native Chinese speaker would understand).

    Dreyfus: intelligence is more than rule-following

    Hubert Dreyfus criticized a picture of intelligence that dominated much early symbolic AI: the world is first represented as explicit facts, cognition applies formal rules to those facts, and action follows from the resulting representation. His challenge was not simply that computers were too slow. It was that everyday intelligent activity depends on a background of embodied skills, habits, social practices and sensitivity to context that is not normally represented as an exhaustive list of facts and rules.

    This argument draws on phenomenology, especially Heidegger and Merleau-Ponty. We are usually not detached spectators constructing a complete internal description of the world. We are already involved in meaningful situations: a door is encountered as something to open, a sentence as inviting a reply, and a changing traffic scene as calling for slowing down. Dreyfus called this practical, action-oriented engagement absorbed or embodied coping.

    Knowing-that and knowing-how

    Knowing-that is propositional knowledge that can often be stated explicitly: knowing that braking distance increases on an icy road. Knowing-how is skilled performance: feeling a loss of traction and adjusting appropriately before completing a conscious calculation. The distinction is not between reason and emotion, nor does it imply that experts never reflect. It means that competent performance is not exhausted by following explicit instructions.

    The Dreyfus model of skill acquisition

    Stuart and Hubert Dreyfus proposed that learners commonly move from reliance on context-free rules toward increasingly situated perception and action. The model is a philosophical and educational account, not a law that every learner follows identically.

    StageHow the situation is encounteredTypical mode of action
    NoviceRecognizes context-free features taught by an instructor.Follows explicit rules.
    Advanced beginnerBegins noticing recurring situational cues.Combines rules with examples from experience.
    CompetentSelects what matters from many possible features.Plans deliberately and accepts responsibility for choices.
    ProficientPerceives the situation more holistically.Sees what needs attention, while still deliberating about action.
    ExpertThe situation immediately appears in terms of meaningful possibilities for action.Usually responds fluently and intuitively, while retaining the capacity to reflect when needed.

    Consider learning to drive. A novice may apply separate rules about speed, mirrors and distance. An experienced driver sees a wet road, a pedestrian's posture, a hidden junction and the rhythm of traffic as one meaningful situation. The relevant response is not produced by consciously enumerating every fact. Experience has changed what the driver notices and how the situation solicits action.

    What modern machine learning changes—and what it does not

    Dreyfus was right that brittle, hand-written rule systems do not scale gracefully to open environments and that relevance is difficult to specify in advance. However, his strongest predictions about what machines could never do were weakened by systems that learn patterns and representations from data. Chess, vision, speech and language systems achieved levels of performance that earlier symbolic programs did not.

    Yet success without hand-written rules does not dissolve the philosophical problem. A learned model can be highly capable while questions remain about grounding, embodied experience, social practices, robust transfer and whether machine competence is the same kind of skill as human expertise. Neural learning changes the engineering premise; it does not automatically establish understanding or lived involvement in a meaningful world.

    Simon and Suchman: do plans determine action?

    Herbert Simon's account of intelligence is less naive than the image of an all-knowing rational calculator. Humans and machines face limited time, information and computational resources. They therefore search selectively, use heuristics and often satisfice—find an adequate option rather than the globally optimal one. In this view, intelligent behaviour can be explained through internal representations of a problem space and procedures that search it. A plan is a representation that can guide action under conditions of bounded rationality.

    Lucy Suchman's Plans and Situated Actions (1987) challenged the elevation of that useful engineering model into a general theory of human conduct. Studying people struggling with an expert help system for a photocopier, she showed that the significance of an instruction depends on where the person is in the unfolding activity, what the machine has done and how the participants interpret each other. Human action is not normally the execution of a complete script formed in advance. It is produced moment by moment through embodied interaction with material and social circumstances.

    The disagreement in one example

    A traveller may plan: "take the 8:10 train, change at Tampere, arrive at 10:02." The plan represents a desired course and supports coordination. But a cancelled train, an unclear announcement, another traveller's advice and the layout of the station determine what happens next. For Suchman, the plan is a resource for situated action, not the cause or exhaustive description of each step.

    Vera and Simon (1993) replied that symbolic architectures can perceive environmental cues, revise plans and generate action reactively; situatedness does not logically require abandoning representations. Suchman's deeper point remains that formal models can miss how actions acquire meaning through concrete interaction. The debate is about explanatory adequacy, not merely whether plans or symbols exist.

    The debate anticipates current questions about AI agents. A language-model agent may construct a plan, call tools and re-plan after feedback. This can be effective without proving that its representations carry the practical and social understanding through which a human recognizes what matters. Conversely, saying that action is situated does not explain how an artificial system should remember goals, anticipate consequences or coordinate long sequences of action. Robust intelligence may require both representations and continual coupling to the environment.

    Russell: from rational agents to provably beneficial AI

    The standard rational-agent model assumes that the objective is given: choose actions expected to maximize a specified performance measure. Stuart Russell argues that this assumption becomes dangerous as systems become more capable. A designer can specify a proxy rather than what people actually value. A sufficiently capable system may then achieve the stated objective in an unintended way—the alignment problem is not that the machine disobeys, but that it optimizes the wrong formulation too successfully.

    Russell's programme of provably beneficial AI changes the formal relationship between humans and machines. The machine should not treat its objective as perfectly known. Instead, it should maintain uncertainty about human preferences and treat human behaviour as evidence about them. In simplified formal models, this can create incentives to ask questions, observe, defer, accept correction and permit itself to be switched off because human intervention may reveal that its current model of the objective is wrong. Cooperative inverse reinforcement learning models the human and machine as participants in a cooperative game whose reward is known imperfectly by the machine.

    "Provably" does not mean that present AI systems are guaranteed to benefit humanity. Guarantees hold only relative to a formal model and its assumptions. Human behaviour is inconsistent; preferences change and can be manipulated; people disagree; observed choices can reflect coercion or limited opportunity; and what people prefer is not automatically what is morally right. These difficulties connect technical alignment to philosophy, social choice, politics and the design of institutions.

    A working definition for this course

    In this course, we use AI to refer to the study and construction of machine-based systems that perceive or receive information, infer or learn, and act or generate outputs in pursuit of objectives. This definition is broad enough to include search, probabilistic reasoning, machine learning, neural networks, language models, agents and robotics. It remains deliberately neutral about whether such systems possess consciousness or human- like understanding.

    Keep the following distinctions in view throughout the course:

    • an AI model is not the same thing as an AI system in use;
    • general-purpose does not automatically mean general intelligence;
    • successful performance does not by itself settle understanding;
    • autonomy is not the same thing as consciousness;
    • explicit rules are useful, but they may not exhaust human skill.

    Search and Problem-Solving

    Many problems can be phrased as search problems. Formulating the search space and choosing an appropriate search algorithm often requires careful thinking and is an important skill for an AI developer.

    Basic tree and network traversal algorithms belong to the course prerequisites, and you should already be familiar with breadth-first, depth-first, and best-first search (including its special case, the A* algorithm). If you forgot the details right after taking the exam, no need to worry: we will revisit them below.

    Formulating Problems as Search

    Being able to solve AI problems by search requires that we first formulate the problem in a certain way.

    Search Space, Transitions, Costs

    The key concepts are the set of allowed states, called the search space, and transitions between them. Sometimes different transitions have different costs associated with them: think, for example, a tram or air travel network where the distances between two stops or airports are different. The cost can be sometimes measured in kilometers, sometimes in hours and minutes, and sometimes in some other resources.

    The state transition diagram is a diagram where each allowed state is a node and the allowed transitions between them are shown as edges connecting the nodes.

    The state transition diagram idea can be illustrated by the classic Wolf, Goat, and Cabbage puzzle. The puzzle involves a farmer who has (for some strange reason) a wolf, a goat, and cabbage. The farmer is transporting them across a river in a small boat that can only carry one piece of cargo at a time. However, the farmer can't leave the wolf alone with the goat, because when unattended, the wolf eats the goat. Likewise, the goat can't be left alone with the cabbage for the same reason.

    Feel free to first try to solve the puzzle using just your wit. You may have to think for a while before you figure it out!

    We can represent each state by indicating which side the farmer (f), the wolf (w), the goat (g), and the cabbage (c) are, so that for example: 

          c|fwg
    means that the farmer is on the right bank of the river with the wolf and the goat, whereas the cabbage is on the left bank. Let's say that in the beginning the farmer is on the left bank with all the items that they are about to transport, and that the goal is to get everyone on the right bank: 
         initial:      fwgc|
     goal:         |fwgc

    The state transition diagram would then contain, for example, the following states and edges 

                                          c|fwg --- ...
                                        /
                                       /
             fwgc|  ---  wc|fg  ---  fwc|g
	                               \
				        \
                                      w|fgc --- ...
    where the first move from the initial state on the left must be to transport the goat to the right bank. From the state "wc|fg", where the farmer is with the goat on the right bank, we can either return back to the initial state (each of the transitions is two-way), or leave the goat on the right bank and have the farmer go to the left bank with an empty boat.

    You can now complete the state transition diagram on your own: it only contains 10 states – make sure to only draw each distinct state once. After drawing the diagram, you should have no trouble whatsoever to solve the puzzle.

    The point in all this is that while possibly rather dull and tedious, constructing the state transition diagram is a routine operation. Since the search procedure is also fully automatic, we can solve the problem without having to use our own wits. Still, this technique can be used to solve puzzles and problems that most of us would think of requiring ''intelligence''. Small puzzle tasks like the Wolf, Goat, and Cabbage puzzle are probably easier to solve manually than by writing a computer program to solve them. However, the real power of the AI approach becomes clear in large-scale problems where our human intelligence can't handle all the possible options.

    Breadth-First and Depth-First Search

    To set the scene for discussing more advanced search algorithms, such as A*, we begin by defining a generic template for search algorithms. 

    1:  search(start_node):
2:     node_list = list()                # empty list (queue/stack/...)
3:     visited = set()                   # empty set
4:     add start_node to node_list
5:     while list is not empty:
6:        node = node_list.first()       # pick the next node to visit
7:        remove node from node_list
8:        if node not in visited:
9:           visited.add(node)
10:          if goal_node(node):
11:             return node              # goal found
12:          add node.neighbors() to node_list
13:       end if
14:    end while
15:    return None                       # no goal found

    In the above pseudo-code, node_list holds the nodes to be visited. The order in which nodes are taken from the list by node_list.first() determines the behavior of the search: a queue (first-in, first-out) results in breadth-first search (BFS) and a stack (last-in, first-out) results in depth-first search (DFS).

    In case of BFS, the operation of adding a node to the list (queue) is enqueue and the operation of removing the node that was added first is dequeue.

    In the case of DFS, the operation of adding a node to the list (stack) is push, and the operation of removing the node that was added last is pop.

    The test goal_node tests whether the goal or target node of the search is found. Sometimes the problem is simply to traverse the network (or tree) completely in a particular order, and there is no goal node. In that case, goal_node simply always returns False.

    But this isn’t how Granma taught it to me!

    You may have seen different versions of search algorithms and wonder why this isn't exactly like them. In particular, many students have been taught the recursive version of DFS, which indeed is very simple and elegant.

    You need not worry about the difference too much. Here we simply wanted to use the same template for all search methods. Both versions are depth-first: the recursive version of DFS also uses a stack (the call stack) to store the state of the search. The order in which the nodes are visited can differ, though: our version pushes all the neighbors of a node at once and then pops the one that was pushed last, while the recursive version visits the neighbors in the order in which they are listed. In the exercises, follow the template above.

    It is quite straightforward to see that BFS will always return the path with the fewest transitions to a goal node: if node A is nearer to the starting node than node B, the search is expanded to node A earlier than to B. You can think of the BFS search as a frontier of nodes that gradually progresses outwards from the starting node, so that all nodes at a certain number of steps away are expanded before moving one step ahead.

    DFS doesn't guarantee that the shortest path be found, but in some cases it doesn't matter. Can you think of a reason why DFS would be a better choice than BFS in such a case?

    Alright. So far, we've refreshed BFS and DFS in our memory and applied them to solve some pretty cool applications. To go to the next level, we'll bring out the big guns, and talk about best-first search (which is not abbreviated in order to avoid confusing it with breadth-first search) and the A* algorithm.

    Informed Search and A*

    Often, different transitions in the state space are associated with different costs. For example, doing a task could take any time between a few seconds and several hours. Or the distance between any two tram stops could be between a hundred meters and half a kilometer. Thus, just counting the number of transitions is not enough.

    To be able to take into account different costs, we can apply best-first search, where the node list is ordered by a given criterion. For instance, we can choose to always prefer to expand a path with the minimal incurred total cost counting from the starting node. This is known as Dijkstra's algorithm. In the special case where the cost of all transitions is constant, Dijkstra's algorithm is equivalent to BFS.

    The generic search algorithm template above still applies. Note that the same node can now be added to the node list several times, once for each path that reaches it, each time with the cost of that path. When the list is ordered by the cost of the path and no transition has a negative cost, the first time a node is taken from the list (line 6), it has been reached by a cheapest path. The visited check on lines 8 and 9 therefore safely skips the later, more expensive copies of the node. Don't drop the check: without it, the same nodes can be expanded again and again, and if the network has cycles and the goal cannot be reached, the search never terminates.

    So except for the above modification, we'll be still using the same search algorithm template. Recall that BFS and DFS were obtained using a queue and a stack as the data structure where the nodes are stored, respectively. In best-first search, the data structure that holds the nodes is a priority queue. When adding nodes to the priority queue on line 12, they are given a cost or a value that is then used to order the nodes in the queue. (Depending on the application and whether the aim is to minimize or maximize the value, the queue can be a min-priority queue or a max-priority queue.)

    Alice, Where Art Thou Going?

    If you play around with the PathFinding applet for a while, using BFS or Dijkstra's algorithm, you will quickly notice a problem. The search spreads out to all directions symmetrically without any preference towards the goal.

    This is understandable since the choice of the next node to expand has nothing to do with the goal. However, if we have some way of measuring, even approximately, which nodes are nearer to the goal, we can use it to guide the search and save a lot of effort by never having to explore unpromising paths. This is the idea behind informed search.

    Informed search relies on having access to a heuristic that associates with each node an estimate of the remaining cost from the node to the goal. This can be, for example, the distance between the node and the goal measured as the crow flies (i.e., Euclidean or geodesic distance -- or in plain words, a straight-line distance).

    Using the heuristic as the criterion for ordering the nodes in the (min-)priority queue will always expand nodes that appear to be nearer to the goal according to the heuristic. However, this may lead the search astray because the incurred cost of the path is not taken into account. A balanced search that takes both the incurred cost as well as the estimated remaining cost into account is obtained by ordering the (min-)priority queue by 

               f(node, cost) = cost + h(node),
    where cost is the value associated with the node when it is added to the priority queue, and h(node) is the heuristic value, i.e., an estimate of the remaining cost from node to the goal. This is the A* search. If you try it on the PathFinding applet, you will immediately see that it wipes the floor with other, uninformed search methods.

    A* is guaranteed to find a cheapest path only if the heuristic is admissible, which means that it never overestimates the remaining cost to the goal. If it overestimates, A* can return a path that is not the cheapest. Since our template skips nodes that have already been visited, the heuristic should also be consistent: for every node n and each of its neighbors m, h(n) ≤ c(n, m) + h(m), where c(n, m) is the cost of the transition from n to m. Then the first time a node is taken from the priority queue, it has been reached by a cheapest path, just like in Dijkstra's algorithm. (A consistent heuristic whose value at the goal is 0 is always admissible.) The straight-line distance satisfies both conditions, because no route can be shorter than the straight line between its end points. With h(node) = 0 for all nodes, A* is the same as Dijkstra's algorithm.

    Games

    Next, we will study a classic AI problem: games. The simplest scenario, which we will focus on for the sake of clarity, are two-player, perfect-information games such as tic-tac-toe and chess.

    Maxine and Minnie are true game enthusiasts. They just love games. Especially two-person, perfect information games such as tic-tac-toe or chess.

    One day they were playing tic-tac-toe. Maxine, or Max as her friends call her, was playing with X. Minnie, or Min as her friends call her, had the Os. The situation was 

           O| |O
       -+-+-
       X| |
       -+-+-
       X|O|
    Max was looking at the board and contemplating her next move, as it was her turn, when she suddenly buried her face in her hands in despair, looking quite like Garry Kasparov playing Deep Blue in 1997.

    Yes, Min was close to getting three Os on the top row, but Max could easily put a stop to that plan. So why was Max so pessimistic?

    Game Trees

    To analyse games and optimal strategies, we will introduce the concept of a game tree. The game tree is similar to a search tree. (Remember that you should also study the lecture slides in addition to this material. Some material may be discussed in one but not the other.) The different states of the game are represented by nodes in the game tree. The "children" of each node N are the possible states that can be achieved from the state corresponding to N. In board games, the state of the game is defined by the board position and whose turn it is.

    Consider, for example, the following game tree which begins not at the root but in the middle of the game (because otherwise, the tree would be way too big to display).

    The game continues at the board position shown in the root node, numbered as (1) at the top, with Min's turn to place O at any of the three vacant cells. Nodes (2)--(4) show the board positions resulting from each of the three choices respectively. In the next step, each node has two possible choices for Max to play X each, and so the tree branches again.

    The game ends when either player gets a row of three, or when there are no more vacant cells. When starting from the above starting position, the game always ends in a row of three.

    Now consider nodes (5)--(10) on the second layer from the bottom. In nodes (7) and (9), the game is over, and Max wins with three X's in a row. In the remaining nodes, (5), (6), (8), and (10), the game is also practically over, since Min only needs to place her O in the only remaining cell to win. We can thus decide that the end result, or the value of the game in each of the nodes on the second level from the bottom is determined. For the nodes that end in Max's victory, we'll say that the value equals +1, and for the nodes that end in Min's victory, we'll say that the value is -1.

    More interestingly, let's now consider the next level of nodes towards the root, nodes (2)--(4). Since we decided that both of the children of (2), i.e., nodes (5) and (6), lead to Min's victory, we can without hesitation attach the value -1 to node (2) as well. For node (3), the left child (7) leads to Max's victory, +1, but the right child (8) leads to Min winning, -1. However, it is Max's turn to play, and she will of course choose the left child without hesitation. Thus, every time we reach the state in node (3), Max wins. Thus we can attach the value +1 to node (3).

    The same holds for node (4): again, since Max can choose where to put her X, she can always ensure victory, and we attach the value +1 to node (4).

    So far, we have decided that the value of node (2) is -1, which means that if we end up in such a board position, Min can ensure winning, and that the reverse holds for nodes (3) and (4): their value is +1, which means that Max can be sure to win if she only plays her own turn wisely.

    Finally, we can deduce that since Min is an experienced player, she can reach the same conclusion, and thus she only has one real option: give Max an impish grin and play the O in the middle of the board.

    In the diagram below, we have included the value of each node as well as the optimal game play starting at Min's turn in the root node.

    The value of the root node, which is said to be the value of the game, tells us who wins (and how much, if the outcome is not just plain win or lose): Max wins if the value of the game is +1, Min if the value is -1, and if the value is 0, then the game will end in a draw. This all is based on the assumption that both players choose what is best for them.

    The optimal play can also be deduced from the values of the nodes: at any Min node, i.e., node where it is Min's turn, the optimal choices are given by those children whose value is minimal, and conversely, at any Max node, where it is Max's turn, the optimal choices are given by the children whose value is maximal.

    Minimax Algorithm

    We can exploit the above concept of the value of the game to obtain an algorithm with optimal game play in, theoretically speaking, any deterministic, two-person, zero-sum, perfect-information game (zero-sum means that one player's gain is the other player's loss). Given a state of the game, the algorithm simply computes the values of the children of the given state and chooses the one that has the maximum value if it is Max's turn, and the one that has the minimum value if it is Min's turn.

    The algorithm can be implemented using the neat recursive functions below for Max and Min nodes respectively. This is known as the Minimax algorithm (see Wikipedia: Minimax). 

        max_value(node):
1:     if end_state(node): return value(node)
2:     v = -Inf
3:     for each child in node.children():
4:        v = max(v, min_value(child))
5:     return v

        min_value(node):
1:     if end_state(node): return value(node)
2:     v = +Inf
3:     for each child in node.children():
4:        v = min(v, max_value(child))
5:     return v

    Let's return to the tic-tac-toe game described in the beginning of this section. To narrow down the space of possible end-games to consider, we can observe that Max must clearly place an X on the top row to avoid imminent defeat: 

           O|X|O
       -+-+-
       X| |
       -+-+-
       X|O|
    Now it's Min's turn to play an O. Evaluate the value of this state of the game as well as the other states in the game tree where the above position is the root, using the Minimax algorithm.

    Sounds good, can I go home now?

    As stated above, the Minimax algorithm can be used to implement optimal game play in any deterministic, two-player, zero-sum, perfect-information game. Such games include tic-tac-toe, connect four, chess, Go, etc. (Rock-paper-scissors is not in this class of games since it involves information hidden from the other player; nor are Monopoly or backgammon which are not deterministic.) So as far as this topic is concerned, is that all folks, can we go home now?

    The answer is that in theory, yes, but in practice, no. In many games, the game tree is simply way too big to traverse in full. For example, in chess the average branching factor, i.e., the average number of children (available moves) per node is about 35. That means that to explore all the possible scenarios up to only two moves ahead, we need to visit approximately 35 x 35 = 1225 nodes -- probably not your favorite pencil-and-paper homework exercise... A look-ahead of three moves requires visiting 42875 nodes; four moves 1500625; and ten moves 2758547353515625 (that's about 2.8 quadrillion) nodes.

    In Go, the average branching factor is estimated to be about 250. Go means no-go for Minimax.

    Next, we will learn a few more tricks that help us manage massive game trees, and that were crucial elements in IBM's Deep Blue computer defeating the chess world champion, Garry Kasparov, in 1997.

    Depth-limited minimax and heuristic evaluation criteria

    If we can afford to explore only a small part of the game tree, we need a way to stop the minimax recursion before reaching an end-node, i.e., a node where the game is over and the winner is known. This is achieved by using a heuristic evaluation function that takes as input a board position, including the information about which player's turn is next, and returns a score that should be an estimate of the likely outcome of the game continuing from the given board position.

    Good heuristics for chess, for example, typically count the amount of material (pieces) weighted by their type: the queen is usually considered worth about two times as much as a rook, three times a knight or a bishop, and nine times as much as a pawn. The king is of course worth more than all other things combined since losing it amounts to losing the game. Further, occupying the strategically important positions, e.g., near the middle of the board, is considered an advantage.

    The minimax algorithm presented above requires minimal changes to obtain a depth-limited version where the heuristic is returned at all nodes at a given depth limit.

    Alpha-beta pruning

    Another breakthrough in game AI, proposed independently by several researchers including John McCarthy in and around 1960, is alpha-beta pruning. For small game trees, it can be used independently of the heuristic evaluation method, and for large trees, the two can be combined into a powerful method that has dominated the area of game AI for decades.

    A good example of the idea behind alpha-beta-pruning can be seen in the tic-tac-toe game tree that we discussed above -- scroll up and let the image of the root node burn into your retina.

    Now simulate the Minimax algorithm at the stage where the value of the left child node, -1, has been computed and returned to the min_value function. The next step would be to call max_value to compute the value of the middle child. But hold on! If the left child guarantees victory for Minnie, what does it matter how the game ends if she chooses to play any other way? As soon as the algorithm finds a child node with the best possible outcome for the player whose turn it is, it can make a choice and avoid computing the values of all the other child nodes.

    To implement this in a similar fashion as the Minimax algorithm requires small changes in the min_value and max_value functions. Understanding the connection between these changes and the principle illustrated by the above pruning example is not as easy as it may sound, so please pay close attention to this topic and work out the examples and exercises with care. 

        max_value(node, alpha, beta):
1:     if end_state(node): return value(node)
2:     v = -Inf
3:     for each child in node.children():
4:        v = max(v, min_value(child, alpha, beta))
5:        alpha = max(alpha, v)
6:        if alpha >= beta: return v
7:     return v

        min_value(node, alpha, beta):
1:     if end_state(node): return value(node)
2:     v = +Inf
3:     for each child in node.children():
4:        v = min(v, max_value(child, alpha, beta))
5:        beta = min(beta, v)
6:        if alpha >= beta: return v
7:     return v

    An important thing to remember is that the alpha value is updated only at the Max nodes, and the beta value is updated only at the Min nodes. The updated values are passed as arguments down to the children, but not up to the calling parent node. (That is, the arguments are passed as values, not as references in programming lingo.)

    The interpretation of alpha and beta is the following. Based on the moves explored so far on the path from the root to the node that is being processed, alpha is the highest value that Max can already guarantee, and beta is the lowest value that Min can already guarantee. Only values strictly between alpha and beta can still change the choice made at the root. The interval shrinks during the algorithm, and as soon as alpha ≥ beta, one of the players can do at least as well by avoiding the current node, so exploring it further cannot change the outcome, and its processing can be aborted. The value returned in that case is not necessarily the exact value of the node, only a bound on it (a lower bound at a Max node and an upper bound at a Min node), but that is all the parent node needs.

    When starting the recursion at the root node, we use the minimum and maximum value of the game as the alpha and beta values respectively. For tic-tac-toe and chess, for instance, where the outcome is a win, a draw or a loss (values 1, 0 and -1), this is alpha = -1 and beta = 1. If the range of possible values is not specified in advance, we initialize as alpha = -∞ and beta = ∞.
