Reasoning under Uncertainty

In this section, we'll study methods that can cope with uncertainty. The history of AI has seen various competing approaches to handling uncertain and imprecise information: fuzzy logic, non-monotonic logic, the theory of plausible reasoning et cetera. In most cases, probability theory has turned out to be the most principled and consistent paradigm for reasoning under uncertainty.

If you want an objective and diplomatic view on the matter, don't ask me. I'll just repeat a counter-argument made by a proponent of fuzzy logic when he tried to get around a rigorous formal proof, known as Cox's theorem, showing that any method of inference where degrees of plausibility are represented as real numbers, and which satisfies a few further requirements (among them, agreement with ordinary two-valued logic), is equivalent to probability theory: the response of the fuzzy logic proponent was that since the proof is based on ordinary (two-valued) logic, it doesn't apply to fuzzy logic. Now that is a counter-argument that is hard to refute! (To be fair, fuzzy logic was designed to model vagueness, that is, degrees of truth, rather than degrees of belief about statements that are either true or false.)

Rolling the Dice (1p)

The lecturer has two dice in his office (true story). One of them is an ordinary die and will yield any result from one to six with equal probability. However, the other die is loaded (true story, again). It will yield a six with probability 1/2 and any other outcome with probability 1/10. He will roll the ordinary die first, and then the loaded one. Calculate the following probabilities. Recall that P(A \mid B) means the conditional probability of A given B.

  1. P(\text{both outcomes are 6})
  2. P(\text{neither outcome is 6})
  3. P(\text{the sum of the outcomes equals 9})
  4. P(\text{the sum of the outcomes equals 9} \mid \text{at least one outcome is 6})
  5. P(\text{at least one outcome is 6} \mid \text{the sum of the outcomes equals 9})

Hint: We know that probability calculus can be confusing, but keep calm and carry on, you'll get the hang of it!

Example solution
  1. Because the outcomes are independent, we can use the product rule to obtain
    P(\text{both outcomes are 6}) = P(\text{first outcome is 6}) \times P(\text{second outcome is 6}) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083
  2. P(\text{neither outcome is 6}) = P(\text{first is not 6}) \times P(\text{second is not 6}) = \frac{5}{6} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{5}{12} \approx 0.417
  3. Let's denote the result of the two dice with the pair (m, n), where m is the outcome of the first (fair) die, and n is the outcome of the second (loaded) die. Together the outcomes define the elementary event. The sum equals nine in the following elementary events: (3, 6), (4, 5), (5, 4), (6, 3). Note that the results (3, 6) and (6, 3) are distinct elementary events.

    The result is obtained by summing up the probabilities of the aforementioned elementary events:
    P((3, 6)) + P((4, 5)) + P((5, 4)) + P((6, 3)) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{10} + \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{10} = \frac{8}{60} \approx 0.133
  4. Denote by A the event "the sum of the outcomes equals 9", and by B the event "at least one outcome is 6". Since the conditional probability is given by P(A \mid B) = P(A, B) / P(B), we start by calculating P(A, B) and P(B).

    The elementary events where both A and B occur are (3, 6) and (6, 3). Thus,
    P(A, B) = \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{6} \times \frac{1}{10} = \frac{6}{60}
    Event B, on the other hand, is the complement of event "neither outcome is 6" from item 2 above, so we can get its probability easiest by applying the rule P(B) = 1 - P(\neg B), to get P(B) = 1 - 5/12 = 7/12.

    Thus we get the conditional probability by substituting into the formula
    P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(B)} = \frac{6/60}{7/12} = \frac{6}{35} \approx 0.171
  5. With the notation of the previous item, we get
    P(B \mid A) = \frac{P(A, B)}{P(A)} = \frac{6/60}{8/60} = \frac{6}{8} = 0.75
    where P(A) = 8/60 was calculated in item 3 above.

Bayes rule (1.5p)

Let's use the same dice as in the previous exercise.

  1. What is the distribution of the sum of the outcomes when rolling the ordinary die twice?
  2. What is the distribution of the sum of the outcomes when rolling the loaded die twice?
  3. Assume that either die (ordinary or loaded) is selected at random with probability 1/2 each. The selected die is rolled twice. Calculate the probability P(\text{the die is loaded} \mid \text{the sum of the outcomes is 10}). Calculate the probability that the die is ordinary as well to make sure that the sum of the two probabilities equals one.
  4. Again, either die is selected with equal probability. We notice that every time the die is rolled, the outcome is six. After how many rolls can we be at least 99% sure that the die is loaded?

    Hint: Calculate the ratio of the posterior probabilities of each die after k rolls. If the posterior probability of the loaded die is over 99%, then the ratio of the posterior probabilities is over 0.99 / 0.01 = 99.

If you have trouble with these exercises, you may wish to study additional material on the Bayes rule. Good pointers are provided above.

Example solution
  1. The "distribution of the outcomes" means a list of probabilities for each possible outcome (here: sums of the two dice). The sum can take any value between 2, ..., 12. With a fair die, each elementary event (m, n) has probability 1/6 \times 1/6 = 1/36, so the distribution is obtained by counting how many elementary events correspond to each possible sum and multiplying this by 1/36:
    \bigl[P(\text{sum is 2}), \ldots, P(\text{sum is 12})\bigr] = \Bigl[\frac{1}{36}, \frac{2}{36}, \frac{3}{36}, \frac{4}{36}, \frac{5}{36}, \frac{6}{36}, \frac{5}{36}, \frac{4}{36}, \frac{3}{36}, \frac{2}{36}, \frac{1}{36}\Bigr]
  2. With the loaded die, the elementary events have different probabilities, so a simple counting and multiplication won't do. Every event (m, n) where m < 6 and n < 6 has probability 1/10 \times 1/10 = 1/100. Every event (m, n) where m < 6 and n = 6, or m = 6 and n < 6, has probability 1/10 \times 1/2 = 1/20. And finally, the event (6, 6) has probability 1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4.

    We now get, for example, the probability that the sum is 9 from
    P((3, 6)) + P((4, 5)) + P((5, 4)) + P((6, 3)) = \frac{1}{20} + \frac{1}{100} + \frac{1}{100} + \frac{1}{20} = \frac{12}{100} = 0.12
    The complete distribution is given by
    \bigl[P(\text{sum is 2}), \ldots, P(\text{sum is 12})\bigr] = \Bigl[\frac{1}{100}, \frac{2}{100}, \frac{3}{100}, \frac{4}{100}, \frac{5}{100}, \frac{14}{100}, \frac{13}{100}, \frac{12}{100}, \frac{11}{100}, \frac{10}{100}, \frac{25}{100}\Bigr]
    To make sure we haven't messed up (who, me?), we can sum up the probabilities to check that the total is 1.0; i.e., 1+2+3+4+5+14+13+12+11+10+25=100. Yes!
  3. Here we can apply the Bayes formula:
    P(\text{loaded die} \mid \text{the sum is 10}) = \frac{P(\text{the sum is 10} \mid \text{loaded die}) \times P(\text{loaded die})}{P(\text{the sum is 10})}
    The first term in the numerator was calculated in the previous item, and the other term is known to be 1/2 by assumption. However, we'll have to calculate the annoying denominator by splitting it into two alternative ways to get the sum 10:
    \begin{aligned} P(\text{sum is 10}) &= P(\text{sum is 10} \mid \text{fair die}) \times P(\text{fair die}) + P(\text{sum is 10} \mid \text{loaded die}) \times P(\text{loaded die}) \\ &= \frac{3}{36} \times \frac{1}{2} + \frac{11}{100} \times \frac{1}{2} = \frac{29}{300} \approx 0.0967 \end{aligned}
    In case you have difficulty following these calculations, please take a look at the additional material on Bayes rule above. Note that the numerical values of both of the conditional probabilities in the above expression come from the two distributions we calculated in items 1 & 2.

    Substituting these values into the Bayes rule we get
    P(\text{loaded die} \mid \text{sum is 10}) = \frac{11/100 \times 1/2}{29/300} = \frac{33}{58} \approx 0.569
    When calculating the conditional probability P(\text{fair die} \mid \text{sum is 10}) by the Bayes rule, we'll end up with the very same denominator, whose value we already calculated to be 29/300, and thus we get:
    P(\text{fair die} \mid \text{sum is 10}) = \frac{3/36 \times 1/2}{29/300} = \frac{25}{58} \approx 0.431
    The sum of the probabilities of the loaded and the fair die is 33/58 + 25/58 = 1 as it should!

    Note: The end result of the above calculation is that observing that the sum is 10 has almost no effect on our belief about which die was chosen: before the observation, our prior probability for the loaded die was 0.5, and after the observation, our posterior probability is 0.569. You can think about what outcome would have changed our belief the most.
  4. Following the hint, it'll be enough to check whether
    \frac{P(\text{loaded die} \mid k \text{ times 6})}{P(\text{fair die} \mid k \text{ times 6})} > 99
    The posterior probability ratio can be obtained by applying the Bayes rule to both of the conditional probabilities above:
    \frac{P(k \text{ times 6} \mid \text{loaded die}) \times P(\text{loaded die}) \,/\, \cancel{P(k \text{ times 6})}}{P(k \text{ times 6} \mid \text{fair die}) \times P(\text{fair die}) \,/\, \cancel{P(k \text{ times 6})}}
    where the annoying denominator cancels. Since the prior probabilities are the same (0.5), they too cancel (which doesn't happen in general). What is left is the ratio of the likelihood terms:
    \frac{P(k \text{ times 6} \mid \text{loaded die})}{P(k \text{ times 6} \mid \text{fair die})} = \frac{(1/2)^k}{(1/6)^k} = 3^k
    The smallest k for which 3^k > 99 is k = 5 (3^4 = 81, 3^5 = 243). Thus, five rolls are required until the posterior probability of the loaded die is greater than 99%.

Probabilistic Inference

Now that the probability basics are in place, we will see how they are used for inference in AI problems.

Bayes rule and probabilistic inference

Bayes rule has a central role in statistical inference and machine learning. The basis for this is that it can be applied in scenarios where one of the random variables in the model corresponds to an unknown "state" which we are interested to learn. If the model includes other variables that correspond to "observations" that can be made and that are dependent on the unknown state, we can use the Bayes rule as follows

\underbrace{P(\text{state} \mid \text{observation})}_{\footnotesize\textsf{posterior probability}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{P(\text{state})}^{\footnotesize\textsf{prior probability}} \;\; \overbrace{P(\text{observation} \mid \text{state})}^{\footnotesize\textsf{likelihood}}} {\underbrace{P(\text{observation})}_{\footnotesize\textsf{the (annoying) denominator}}}

The left side of the equation is called the posterior probability (probability after the observation). The first factor in the numerator is called the prior probability (probability prior to the observation), the second factor is called the likelihood (how likely the observation is when the state is given), and the denominator has many names, of which the annoying denominator may be the most fitting. (Evidence and marginal likelihood are more common.)

On (not) calculating the annoying denominator

As you may have noticed in the dice exercises above, and as you will notice again later this week, calculating the denominator may indeed be annoying.

A nice trick that can sometimes save a lot of effort is to calculate the ratio of posterior probabilities, instead of the probabilities themselves. For example, consider the following ratio

R = \frac{P(\text{State} = 1 \mid \text{obs})}{P(\text{State} = 2 \mid \text{obs})}

where obs is an abbreviation for observation. Applying Bayes rule to both the numerator and the denominator in the ratio, we'll notice that the denominator P(\text{obs}) cancels:

R = \frac{P(\text{State} = 1)\, P(\text{obs} \mid \text{State} = 1) \,/\, \cancel{P(\text{obs})}} {P(\text{State} = 2)\, P(\text{obs} \mid \text{State} = 2) \,/\, \cancel{P(\text{obs})}} = \frac{P(\text{State} = 1)\, P(\text{obs} \mid \text{State} = 1)}{P(\text{State} = 2)\, P(\text{obs} \mid \text{State} = 2)}

In case the two states (1 and 2) are the only possibilities, we have P(\text{State} = 2 \mid \text{obs}) = 1 - P(\text{State} = 1 \mid \text{obs}) (because one of the events must happen and both cannot occur at the same time). In this case, after calculating the above ratio, R, it can be mapped back into the posterior probability of state 1 by

P(\text{State} = 1 \mid \text{obs}) = \frac{R}{1 + R}

You can check that this is true by substituting the fact P(\text{State} = 2 \mid \text{obs}) = 1 - P(\text{State} = 1 \mid \text{obs}) into the definition of R.

Naive Bayes classification

One of the most useful applications of the Bayes rule is the so called naive Bayes classifier. It is a machine learning technique that can be used to classify objects such as text documents into two or more classes. The classifier is trained by analysing a set of training data, for which the correct classes are given.

The naive Bayes model is a probabilistic model that involves a class variable -- this corresponds to the state variable above -- and a number of feature variables. The assumption in the model is that the feature variables are conditionally independent given the class. (We will not discuss the exact meaning of conditional independence on this course. You can find more about it from the literature. For our purposes, it is enough to be able to exploit conditional independence in building the classifier.)

We will use a spam email filter as a running example for illustrating the idea of the naive Bayes classifier. Thus, the class variable indicates whether a message is spam (or "junk email") or whether it is a legitimate message (also called "ham"). The words in the message correspond to the feature variables, so that the number of feature variables in the model is determined by the length of the message.

The naive Bayes model can be represented as a Bayesian network, which encodes the conditional independence between the feature variables (in this case, the words of the email message) given the class variable as in the above diagram. We'll return to Bayesian networks in Week 3.

Estimating parameters

To define the naive Bayes model, we need to specify the distribution of each variable. For the class variable, this is the distribution of spam vs ham messages, which we can for simplicity assume to be 1:1, i.e., P(\text{spam}) = P(\text{ham}) = 0.5.

For the feature variables, we will define two distributions: one for the spam messages and another one for the ham messages. In each case, we will make the simplifying assumption that each of the words in the message is distributed according to the same distribution. And as we said above, we also assume that the words are independent of each other given the spam/ham class.

The word distributions for the two classes are best estimated from actual training data, i.e., a corpus of spam messages and a corpus of legitimate messages. The simplest way is to count how many times each word, aardvark, aardwolf, ..., Zyzzogeton, appears in the corpus and dividing the number by the total number of words in the corpus.

To illustrate the idea, let's assume that we have at our disposal both a spam corpus and a ham corpus. You can easily obtain one by saving a batch of your emails in two files.

Assume that we have calculated the number of occurrences of the following words in the two classes of messages:

total95791306438
Watch out for the Singularity!

One problem with estimating the probabilities directly from the counts is that the zero counts lead to zero estimates. This can be quite harmful for the performance of the classifier -- it easily leads to situations where the ratio of the posterior probabilities obtained as 0/0. That sounds dangerously close to the Singularity, so we'd better find a better way. The simplest solution is to use a small lower bound for all probability estimates. The value 0.000001, for instance, does the job.

We can now estimate that the probability that a word in a spam message is million, for example, is about 156 / 95791 \approx 0.0016285. In other words, on the average, roughly every 614th word in a spam message is million. Likewise, we get the estimate 0.0003198 for the probability that a word in a ham message is million. Both of these probability estimates are small but more importantly, the former is higher than the latter. This turns out to be a sign that the word in question hints towards the message being spam -- which sounds logical. Words for which the ratio of the probabilities is the other way around, hint towards the message being ham.

Classifying new data

After the parameters of the model have been estimated, the classifier is ready for use! When we are given a new message, we will compute the posterior probability P(\text{spam} \mid \text{message}), where message is a place-holder for the words in the message, for example, P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’}) (which would be a really short message).

Let's compute the posterior probability

P(\text{spam} \mid \text{Word}_1 = \text{‘million’}, \text{Word}_2 = \text{‘conferences’})

which is abbreviated without a great risk of confusion -- but please note that unlike million and conferences, which are quoted ('like this') to indicate that they are words that occur in the message, the term spam indicates the class of the message, not the word "spam" -- as

P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’})

By Bayes rule, we have

P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’}) = \frac{P(\text{spam})\, P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’})}

The numerator on the right side is the product of the prior probability P(\text{spam}) and the likelihood term P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam}), whose meaning is the probability that the first two words are million and conferences given that the message is spam. The denominator on the right side -- it's the annoying one -- is the probability that the first two words are as stated when the spam/ham status of the message is not given.

Let's look at the likelihood term first. The conditional independence assumption, i.e., the naive Bayes assumption, implies that the likelihood can be "factorized" as follows

P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam}) = P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{spam})\, P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam})

This is very useful since these are the kind of probabilities we have estimated from the data. The same factorization rule can be applied no matter how many words there are in the message.

Next up is the annoying denominator. However, we'll use the hint above and avoid calculating the denominator (at least explicitly). To achieve this, we consider the ratio of posteriors:

\frac{P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’})}{P(\text{ham} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’})} = \frac{P(\text{spam})\, P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam}) \,/\, \cancel{Z}}{P(\text{ham})\, P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{ham}) \,/\, \cancel{Z}}

where Z = P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’}) is the annoying denominator which cancels out just as promised in the hint.

The term P(\text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{ham}) is treated in the exact same manner as the corresponding term for spam (see above), and the numbers required to calculate its value are available since we have estimated them from the training data.

Naive Bayes and Spam (pencil-and-paper) (1p)

Consider the word counts given in the table above (million, dollars, etc).

Use the obtained estimates to calculate the probability P(\text{Word} \neq \text{‘million’}), i.e., the probability that a single word in a message is not million when the class of the message is unknown.     wordspamham

    \begin{aligned} R &= \frac{P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘dollars’}, \text{‘adclick’}, \text{‘conferences’})}{P(\text{ham} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘dollars’}, \text{‘adclick’}, \text{‘conferences’})} \\ &= \frac{P(\text{spam})\, P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{spam})\, P(\text{‘dollars’} \mid \text{spam})\, P(\text{‘adclick’} \mid \text{spam})\, P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{ham})\, P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{ham})\, P(\text{‘dollars’} \mid \text{ham})\, P(\text{‘adclick’} \mid \text{ham})\, P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{ham})} \\ &= \frac{0.5 \times 0.001629 \times 0.000303 \times 0.000532 \times 0.000001}{0.5 \times 0.000320 \times 0.000388 \times 0.000001 \times 0.000039} \approx 54.23 \end{aligned}

    P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘dollars’}, \text{‘adclick’}, \text{‘conferences’}) \approx \frac{54.23}{1 + 54.23} \approx 0.982

Implementation details

The above exercise should make it relatively straightforward to see how the filter can be implemented by programming: the key insight is the routine nature of the word-by-word calculations.

A convenient way to group the computations is obtained by slight reshuffling of the terms. Consider still our example two-word message, and the following formula for the ratio of the posterior probabilities

\begin{aligned} R &= \frac{P(\text{spam} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’})}{P(\text{ham} \mid \text{‘million’}, \text{‘conferences’})} \\[6pt] &= \frac{P(\text{spam})\, P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{spam})\, P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{ham})\, P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{ham})\, P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{ham})} \end{aligned}

Since the ratio of products is equal to the product of ratios – i.e., for example, ABC/(DEF) = (A/D) \cdot (B/E) \cdot (C/F) – we can group the above terms as

R = \frac{P(\text{spam})}{P(\text{ham})} \cdot \frac{P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{ham})} \cdot \frac{P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{‘conferences’} \mid \text{ham})}

More generally, the formula is

R = \frac{P(\text{spam})}{P(\text{ham})} \prod_{i=1}^{N} \frac{P(\text{word}_i \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{word}_i \mid \text{ham})}

where N is the number of words in the message, and \text{word}_i is the ith word in the message.

Intuitively, the above formula encodes the rule that any word whose probability is higher in spam messages than in ham messages will increase the ratio R, and vice versa. For example, in our two-word message, for i = 1, we have \text{word}_1 = \text{‘million’}, and the ratio of the word probabilities is given by

\frac{P(\text{word}_1 \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{word}_1 \mid \text{ham})} = \frac{P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{‘million’} \mid \text{ham})} \approx \frac{0.0016285}{0.0003198} \approx 5.1

Thus, each occurrence of the word 'million' in the message leads to roughly a five-fold increase in the posterior probability ratio.

Putting things together, here's a pseudocode sketch of the classification stage where we assume that the probability estimates have already been calculated from training data. 

    spamicity(words, estimates):
1:     R = estimates.prior_spam / estimates.prior_ham
2:     for each w in words:
3:        R = R * estimates.spam_prob(w) / estimates.ham_prob(w)
4:     return R

Here estimates is an object that stores the estimated probabilities: estimates.prior_spam is the probability that a message is spam, P(\text{spam}), estimates.prior_ham the same for ham; estimates.spam_prob(w) is the probability P(\text{Word} = w \mid \text{spam}), and estimates.ham_prob(w) the same for ham.

Avoiding under- and overflows

When dealing with long messages, the posterior probability ratio becomes a product with very many terms. This can easily lead to trouble because products are creatures that easily grow very very large or very very small (close to zero); the mathematical term is 'exponential rate'.

In order to reduce the risk of under- and overflows in the floating point arithmetics, it is a good idea to carry out the computations in log-scale. That is, instead of computing the ratio, R, you should compute its logarithm, \log R. (Store the log-ratio in a variable like logR.)

The important thing to recall about logarithms is that they convert multiplication into addition, \log(AB) = \log A + \log B, and similarly, division becomes subtraction, \log(A/B) = \log A - \log B. Using these, we can write the log-ratio as

\log R = \log P(\text{spam}) - \log P(\text{ham}) + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl[\log P(\text{word}_i \mid \text{spam}) - \log P(\text{word}_i \mid \text{ham})\bigr]

The base of the logarithm (natural, 2, 10, ...) is not consequential. The important thing is to map the end result back to the ratio by exponentiating the log-ratio with the same base. So if you use natural logarithms, for instance, then you should exponentiate the log-ratio by exp(logR) to get R.

Smoothing done properly

The lower bound of 10^{-6} used in the exercises works, but it is a patch. The standard remedy for zero counts is additive smoothing: pretend that every word of the vocabulary V was seen \alpha extra times in each class,

\hat P(w \mid c) = \frac{n_{w,c} + \alpha}{N_c + \alpha\,|V|},

where n_{w,c} is the number of times word w occurs in class c and N_c is the total number of words in that class. With \alpha = 1 this is Laplace smoothing. It is Bayes rule once more: the estimate is the posterior mean of the word probabilities under a uniform (Dirichlet) prior. A word that never occurs in ham, such as adclick, now gets a small probability instead of zero; with a vocabulary of 50,000 words it is 1 / (306438 + 50000) \approx 2.8 \times 10^{-6}. 

def word_probability(word, counts, total, vocabulary_size, alpha=1.0):
    """Additively smoothed estimate of P(word | class)."""
    return (counts.get(word, 0) + alpha) / (total + alpha * vocabulary_size)

From log-odds back to a probability, safely

The spam filter computes \log R, the logarithm of the posterior odds. Since R / (1 + R) = 1 / (1 + e^{-\log R}), the probability is the logistic (sigmoid) function of the log-odds, the same function that you will meet in logistic regression in Week 3. Written naively it can still fail: Python's math.exp raises OverflowError above about 709, so 1 / (1 + math.exp(-log_r)) crashes for log_r = -1000. Evaluate it on the side where the exponential is small: 

import math

def spam_probability(log_r):
    """P(spam | message) from log R, the log of the posterior odds, without overflow."""
    if log_r >= 0:
        return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-log_r))
    odds = math.exp(log_r)          # at most 1, so it cannot overflow
    return odds / (1.0 + odds)

With more than two classes, such as the ten digits of the naive Bayes classifier in Week 3, the same idea is called the log-sum-exp trick: subtract the largest log score before exponentiating. The scores -1000, -1002, -1010 all underflow to 0 with math.exp, so normalising them directly divides 0 by 0; shifted by the maximum they give the probabilities 0.881, 0.119 and 0.00004. The softmax in the attention layers of Week 5 is computed in exactly this way. 

def posterior_from_logs(log_scores):
    """Class probabilities from log scores (log prior + sum of log likelihoods)."""
    top = max(log_scores)
    weights = [math.exp(score - top) for score in log_scores]   # largest: exp(0) = 1
    total = sum(weights)
    return [weight / total for weight in weights]

Why "naive" Bayes works better than its assumption

Words are obviously not independent given the class: "click" and "here" travel together. Yet naive Bayes classifies well. Domingos and Pazzani (1997) showed why: a classifier only needs the largest posterior to be in the right place, and naive Bayes is optimal under zero-one loss in a far larger set of situations than the ones where its independence assumption holds. The price is in the probabilities. Correlated words are counted as independent evidence, so the posteriors pile up near 0 and 1; the underflow figure above reports P(\text{spam} \mid \text{message}) = 1 - 1.97 \times 10^{-14} after only 60 words, which no honest model of email should claim. Use the ranking, not the number, or calibrate the probabilities on held-out data.

Two puzzles for your intuition

The rare disease. One person in a thousand has a disease. A test detects it 99% of the time, and gives a false positive for 5% of healthy people. You test positive. How likely is it that you have the disease? Guess first, then calculate, and check with the interactive figure above (set P(condition) to 0.1%).

Show the answer
P(\text{disease} \mid +) = \frac{0.001 \times 0.99}{0.001 \times 0.99 + 0.999 \times 0.05} = \frac{0.00099}{0.05094} \approx 0.019

About 2%. In natural frequencies: of 100,000 people, 100 are ill and 99 of them test positive, but so do 4,995 of the 99,900 healthy people. Only 99 of the 5,094 positives are ill. The prior matters.

Monty Hall. A car is behind one of three doors and goats are behind the others. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the car is, always opens a door that you did not pick and that hides a goat, choosing at random when he can choose. He opens door 3. Should you switch to door 2?

Show the answer

Yes. The prior is 1/3 for each door. The likelihood of the host opening door 3 is 1/2 if the car is behind door 1, 1 if it is behind door 2 (he can open neither door 2 nor your door 1), and 0 if it is behind door 3. Bayes rule gives

P(\text{car behind 2} \mid \text{door 3 opened}) = \frac{(1/3) \cdot 1}{(1/3) \cdot (1/2) + (1/3) \cdot 1 + (1/3) \cdot 0} = \frac{1/3}{1/2} = \frac23 .

Switching wins two times out of three. Note the annoying denominator: the probability that the host opens door 3 at all, which is 1/2.
